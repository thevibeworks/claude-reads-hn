*** TLDR
WallHop is a free paywall bypass: paste an article URL, or prefix any URL with wallhop.io/, and it chains several bypass methods to return the text without the paywall overlay, ads or signup. There is an iPhone shortcut, a bookmarklet, and /raw/ and /api/ endpoints. It is a modified fork of everywall/ladder, which is GPL-3.0, and the fork is not public yet. *** Take
The code is the least interesting part. What the thread actually exposes is that the author shipped a closed modification of GPL-3 software while telling readers to go subscribe, and that the paywalls being defeated are the kind that ship the full article to your browser and then hide it. Both complaints in the thread are correct, which is why nobody in 90 comments convinced anybody.

** Unikernels were hard. key word: were :unikernels:ocaml:ai:infrastructure:
:PROPERTIES:
:ID:       50033357
:URL:      https://ghuntley.com/unikernels/
:HN_URL:   https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50033357
:POINTS:   129
:COMMENTS: 55
:BY:       ghuntley
:END:

*** TLDR
Geoffrey Huntley argues that unikernels, where the application is compiled as its own operating system with no separate userland, were hard for reasons that coding agents now remove: missing libraries, unfamiliar tooling, and the dogma that Nix, Bazel and MirageOS are too much work to learn. Instead of writing a Stripe client for OCaml, you run an agent in a loop to port the Go one. He cites roughly $6,000 of Claude usage over three months to build his toy language Cursed, and a million-line Rust S3 clone built with four agents compiling at once. Named platforms: MirageOS, seL4, Unikraft.

*** Take
The post is honest about what actually changed: nothing in unikernel technology, only the price of writing the missing pieces. That is a real shift, and it is also the weakest possible case for running one in production, which is why the sharpest reply is an SRE noting that the observability surface shrinks along with the attack surface. You lose strace, eBPF, lsof and ss, and the post never prices that in.

*** TLDR
WerWolv documents stripping three layers of copy protection off "Findus bei den Mucklas," a German point-and-click game from the early 2000s, so it runs without the CD. The disc holds duplicate directory entries where one copy is 0 or 256 bytes of garbage to confuse extraction tools, worked around with ccd2iso plus manual file picking. The executable turns out to be a Macromedia Director projector with a .dxr appended at offset 0x002222F0, disassembled with ProjectorRays after the author contributed an --offset flag; the recovered Lingo bytecode scans drives D through Z for a "p3media" folder and calls quit() if it is absent, defeated by changing one jmpifz operand from 95 00 60 to 95 00 03. The third layer, OPTGRAPH.DLL, queries the physical disc with low-level CD commands expecting the value 26079537, patched in Ghidra to MOV EAX, 26079537; RET. The whole sequence is scripted in Python with SHA-256 checks before each patch.

*** Take
This is what archival work looks like when someone does it properly: three unrelated protection schemes, three different techniques, and a Python script with hash checks so the next person can reproduce it instead of trusting a forum attachment. The Lingo layer came down to one operand byte, which is a fair measure of how much of 2002's anti-piracy engineering survives contact with a disassembler.
