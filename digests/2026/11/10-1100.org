** Amazon saves millions by replacing Sonnet 4.6 with Strands Decider :ai:llm:aws:classification:cost:

*** TLDR
An Amazon internal team replaced Claude Sonnet 4.6 on Bedrock with Strands Decider, the E4B Gemma 4 build, running on SageMaker, to label tickets into one of 20 categories. Instead of asking a model to emit JSON, they condensed each label into a one or two sentence criterion and posed the whole thing as a single multiple choice question with a 'none' option; the model returns a probability per option. Cost per million tickets fell from about $22,200 to about $500, average latency from 4,215 ms to 897 ms, tokens per ticket from 6,724 to 2,010, and Lambda duration by 74%. The 4,096-token window forces them to drop the oldest comments on long tickets, and they measured output probabilities shifting by up to 0.19 depending on JSON key ordering. The authors state the approach only works for a closed choice from a known catalog at high volume.

*** Take
The 44x saving is real, but the headline buries the actual finding: they were paying a frontier model to do 20-way classification, which has had cheap answers for a decade. armcat shipped the same pipeline on SVMs with minutes of training and millisecond inference. The questions the thread raised and the post does not answer are better than the post: nobody tried Haiku 5.5, and Amazon already owns millions of labeled tickets, so a vendor decider model is a strange place to end up. The 0.19 probability swing from reordering JSON keys is not a footnote either, it is a calibration defect in the thing now routing tickets.

*** TLDR
maurycyz hides readable ASCII text inside the RSA modulus of a working TLS certificate. The method: generate two random primes, multiply them, overwrite bytes in the upper half of the product so its base64 encoding spells a message, then recompute q as n divided by p and round up to the next prime. Because a small change in q only affects the least significant half of the product, the message in the top half survives. Let's Encrypt signs the result through ordinary ACME, since nothing in the issuance path inspects the structure of a submitted public key. About 150 characters fit in an RSA-2048 key, and the certificate still serves real TLS for the site, with forward secrecy covering past sessions even if the weakened key were broken.

*** Take
Nothing is broken here, which is the interesting part: a public key is an opaque integer to every piece of software that touches it, so any structure you hide in it travels all the way through a CA unexamined. The reason to care is not this certificate, it is miki123211's extension of the idea to Certificate Transparency, where a public append-only log that accepts attacker-controlled bytes at kilobyte scale is a storage medium nobody designed and nobody can delete from. tptacek is right that unauthenticated TLS is not secure, but that objection lands on the post's framing, not on the trick.

***** Title

***** TLDR
