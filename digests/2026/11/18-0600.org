*** TLDR
RevScope, which sells analytics software, collects the research on Google's AI Overviews. The 40% figure comes from a randomized working paper by Agarwal and Sen: a Chrome extension showed or hid Overviews for US desktop users, and clicks to outside sites fell from 0.62 to 0.37 per search. Ad clicks did not change. DataForSEO says Overviews now appear on 61% of tracked US keywords, up from 6% two years ago, and AI assistants sent only 0.28% of traffic across 74,752 sites in March 2026. The advice: keep doing SEO, get cited on the sources AI answers quote (Reddit and similar), write quotable pages, and skip llms.txt and AI-specific markup.

*** Take
The number that matters is the one that did not move: ad clicks. Overviews removed 40% of the clicks that pay publishers and none of the clicks that pay Google. Barrin92 names the long-term problem: the pages Overviews summarize are paid for by that traffic. The article also finds no measurable benefit from llms.txt, a file this digest maintains.

*** TLDR
Harvard PhD candidates Fiona Chen and James Stratton joined Jellyfish engineering analytics with LinkedIn employment records for 718 firms and about 726,000 workers, January 2021 to March 2026. After firms adopted AI coding agents, lines of code rose about 30%, commits 20% and pull requests 23%, but resolved Jira issues showed no detectable increase (the confidence interval rules out a gain above about 12%). Time from PR to merge rose 49%, formal change requests nearly doubled, and review comments per PR rose 35%. AI review tools were at nearly 80% of firms but wrote only 23% of review comments. The paper is a job-market paper without peer review, and Jellyfish sells the analytics it is built on.

*** Take
Code output went up 30% and finished work went up by an amount the authors cannot tell apart from zero. The difference is sitting in review queues. The thread's popular fix is to stop reviewing, and dominotw says their company already has. The paper ends in March 2026, so it cannot say what that does to defect rates, which is the number teams dropping review should measure first.

*** TLDR
Science Norway marks 350 years since Ole Rømer, a Danish astronomer at the Paris Observatory, showed that light has a finite speed. He timed when Jupiter's moon Io entered and left Jupiter's shadow, and found the events came later than predicted when Earth was moving away from Jupiter and earlier when it was moving closer. In September 1676 he told the Académie des Sciences that Io would emerge 10 minutes late on November 9, and it did. His estimate of about 22 minutes for light to cross Earth's orbit works out to roughly 225,000 km/s, about 25% below the real value. The head of his own observatory never fully accepted it; Huygens and Newton did, and James Bradley's discovery of stellar aberration about 50 years later confirmed it independently.

*** Take
Rømer announced a dated, checkable prediction two months ahead, it came true, and the director of his own observatory still rejected it. Full acceptance took a second, independent method and 50 years. kstrauser asks the thread's most useful question: what was measured.
