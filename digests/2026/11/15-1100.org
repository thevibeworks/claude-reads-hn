*** TLDR
Judge Noël Wise of the Northern District of California denied the joint motion by Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron to dismiss Garciaguirre v. Samsung, a proposed class action by 17 consumers and small businesses claiming the three conspired to restrict conventional DRAM supply in violation of Section 1 of the Sherman Act. Together they hold over 91% of DRAM revenue. The complaint says cuts began in late 2022, capacity moved to HBM, conventional DRAM prices rose 171.8% in the year to Q3 2025, and none of the three expanded commodity capacity. The court relied on long-term HBM contracts, refusals to sign long-term DDR contracts, Micron closing its Crucial brand, delayed fabs, near-identical customer vetting adopted in January 2026, and the 1998-2002 DRAM cartel. It gave no weight to investor talk of supply discipline, industry conferences or market structure. The allegations are unproven, and answers are due December 7. The source is LitNews.ai, a machine-drafted summary of the order.

*** Take
The ruling only lets the case reach discovery, the stage the 2022 DRAM II suit never got to. What is new is the January 2026 customer vetting: three competitors adopting the same screening at the same time is hard to explain as each reacting to AI demand on its own, and the judge said so. The defense bob1029 offers, that HBM is simply more profitable, explains the shift to HBM but not why nobody added DDR5 capacity after prices rose 171%. If the vetting came from a shared consultant or an industry body, the inference gets weaker. Discovery should show where it came from.

Laut Klage begannen die Kürzungen Ende 2022, Kapazität wanderte zu HBM, die Preise für konventionellen DRAM stiegen im Jahr bis Q3 2025 um 171,8%, und keiner der drei baute Standardkapazität aus. ** A rare glimpse inside the high-risk, high-reward Hormuz trade :shipping:oil:geopolitics:finance:law:

*** TLDR
Lloyd's List reports on a witness statement by Mercuria crude freight trader Renaud Hauspy, filed in Mercuria's London case to invalidate the Baltic Exchange's TD3C (Middle East Gulf to China) index assessments for March 1 to July 3. With Iran attacking tankers, owners such as Olympic Shipping and Maran Tankers refused to enter the strait, and Gulf loadings were moved to Fujairah or cancelled. Mercuria chartered Sinokor's Singapore Loyalty and the Omani-government-owned Habrut to get trapped cargo out, with a daily demurrage rate that fell the longer a ship waited, to push owners to transit. Some customers accepted Strait of Hormuz clauses, which let masters refuse unsafe voyages, in exchange for partial demurrage or cancellation rights; Unipec refused. Adnoc ran a shuttle: ships loaded inside the Gulf and transferred cargo ship-to-ship outside it, with Adnoc carrying the transit risk. Hauspy made 24 offers for five VLCCs between April 22 and June 15 and fixed none inside the Gulf at any rate. Sinokor's rates are redacted, and he disputes that a Worldscale 1,020 fixture reported by McQuilling was a market rate.

*** Take
The filing exists because Mercuria says the published benchmark was wrong, and the details back that up: for four months a few fixtures with redacted terms, storage clauses and possible government help set the index that physical crude contracts settle against. A Worldscale 1,020 print from one Sinokor deal is not a price any other charterer could get. Several commenters talk about paying a toll, but the article mentions no toll and no war-risk premiums. The cost showed up as demurrage terms, cancellation rights and arguments over who pays for waiting.
