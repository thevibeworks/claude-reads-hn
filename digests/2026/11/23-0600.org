*** TLDR
REVISIT (48 comments last time, 366 now). OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki publishes the log-perplexity of OpenAI base models since GPT-3.5, measured on a fixed held-out snapshot of OpenAI's internal codebase. He ties capabilities to loss levels: GPT-3.5 at about 0.99, GPT-4 at about 0.91, below 0.8 for serious cybersecurity and math ability, about 0.76 for the model that started producing results on open math problems, and new capabilities expected around 0.68 to 0.70. He proposes that labs report the number and agree on shared limits they will not cross until monitoring improves, for example a successor to chain-of-thought monitors. What the larger thread added: an OpenAI employee said the correlation is empirical and the company will switch metrics if it breaks, and several commenters pointed out the chart implies nearly every release came with a new or extended pretrain, not only better post-training on one base model.

*** Take
The most informative part of the post turns out to be the chart, not the safety proposal. It shows OpenAI's pretraining improving steadily during the period when the public story was that gains came from RL. The proposal still has the problem from last time: the dataset is OpenAI's private code, so nobody can check another lab's number against it. One commenter measured Inkling 2 Mini on their own text and got a figure that would land between GPT-6.2 Astra and OpenAI's internal model. That comparison means nothing, and it shows why a shared limit needs a shared dataset first.

A setup guide from GPUPartner, a site that also sells GPUs and workstations. It installs Unsloth Desktop on Windows with an RTX 5090 (32GB VRAM), downloads Qwen3.8-27B in Unsloth's UD-Q4_K_XL quantization (about 18GB), sets a 128K context, and then turns on Unsloth's built-in LAN sharing, protected by a password, so everyone at home can use it from a browser. Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling 2 Mini (26.4B parameters, 5.4B active, Apache 2.0, 1M context) fits the same card at 17.6GB; Inkling 2 Small (594B) needs a 256GB Mac Studio or a DGX Station. *** TLDR
A one-minute post by Daniel Sada. At Snowflake, he says, broad emails opened with a line like 'If you're not part of the finance org, you can stop reading', and Slack announcements were 3-5 line summaries with the details moved into the thread. He argues writers should say up front who a piece is for, keep it short, and stop posting 2,000-word announcements that apply to few readers. Two screenshots contrast a short Slack post with a long one full of code blocks. In the comments the author, posting as dshacker, said several times that he meant workplace email and Slack more than blogs.

*** Take
The advice is right for company Slack and does not fit the title. A message posted to a whole channel takes space from everyone in it, so a scoping line and a short summary are basic courtesy. A blog post costs nothing to anyone who does not click, and most of the thread agreed bloggers should write for whoever shows up. The post follows its own rule, opening with 'If you do not write for other people, stop reading this now.' The title should have done the same and said 'Slack message' instead of 'blog post'.
