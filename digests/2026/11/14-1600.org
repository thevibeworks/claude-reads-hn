*** TLDR
EFF argues that always-on wearables turn privacy into a problem for the people around the wearer. It names Meta and Google smart glasses, Apple's Audio Intelligence on the Apple Watch, and Amazon's Bee, which transcribes nearly everything and ties into Amazon's store. Each company's safeguards (Apple's white paper on data separation, Meta's face blurring, Amazon's promise that no audio is stored) protect the owner, not the bystander, who has no way to opt out. EFF says owners are expected to know each device's indicators and state recording laws, which is not how consent works. It also quotes a Google executive calling bystander alerts "weirdly disempowering" for the wearer. Its advice: send Apple feedback while the feature is in beta, challenge wearers politely, report abusive videos, and record only visibly and with consent.

*** Take
Illniyar is right that US law gives you no expectation of privacy in public. The change EFF describes is scale: a raised phone is visible and records one moment, while a pendant that logs every conversation all day is neither. EFF's remedies are all social pressure, which is the same thing that killed Google Glass. tdeck's quote from an internal Meta memo, planning the launch for a time when civil society groups would be busy with other fights, shows Meta expected that pressure and timed the launch around it.

***** TLDR

La cita de tdeck de un memorando interno de Meta, que planeaba el lanzamiento para cuando los grupos de la sociedad civil estuvieran ocupados en otras batallas, muestra que Meta contaba con esa presión y eligió el momento para esquivarla.

*** TLDR
［Reuters blocked; based on Rapporteur's report of the same decision.］ Italy's data protection authority fined Meta EUR 805 million (about $887M) because the Facebook app for Android accessed photos, videos and their metadata "without obtaining specific consent and without properly informing" users. The feature is gallery suggestions, live since 2023, which proposes photos from your phone as post ideas. Facebook asked for one gallery permission covering the whole app, preceded by a generic notice about other features, so users could not tell they were also turning on suggestions. About 20 million Italian Android users were affected. The regulator cited Meta's lack of cooperation and says the violation continues for users on old app versions. Meta must now show a separate prompt that states the feature reads the gallery and explains how to turn it off. The fine is about 0.4% of Meta's 2025 turnover. Meta had not commented and may appeal.

*** Take
The thread split over whether users consented, and both sides have a point. Android's permission dialog grants an app the gallery. It has no way to ask which feature the access is for, and GDPR requires consent for each purpose. The regulator's position is that a single "allow" tapped for one feature does not cover Meta scanning your camera roll to suggest posts. At 0.4% of turnover the fine changes nothing at Meta. The separate-prompt order is the part that will change what Italian users see.
