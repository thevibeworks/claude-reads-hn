*** TLDR
Adam Wathan announced on September 9 that Tailwind Labs is joining Shopify. Tailwind CSS stays MIT-licensed and community-led, with Shopify funding maintenance. The commercial side is being wound down: existing Tailwind Plus and ui.sh customers keep their access, but new signups are closed. Terms were not disclosed, and the post gives three reasons for the move, none of them financial: Shopify is a large real application to build against, it was an early large-scale Tailwind adopter, and Wathan believes in its mission. Tailwind is installed over 110 million times a week.

*** Take
The announcement never mentions why a project with 110 million weekly installs needed a buyer. Wathan already said it in January: 75 percent of the engineering team was laid off and docs traffic is down 40 percent from early 2023. Selling templates and docs-adjacent products stops working when the docs get read by models instead of people. This is the exit for a business whose product is free and whose distribution got eaten.

*** TLDR
Apple's first foldable iPhone: a 7.6-inch inner Super Retina XDR display that Apple says is 50 percent larger than the iPhone 18 Pro Max, a 5.4-inch outer screen, an A20 Pro with dual 16-core Neural Engine, dual 48MP Fusion cameras, a Grade 5 titanium frame and IP68. Apple credits optically clear adhesives that let the display layers slide as the screen folds for minimizing the crease. Dual batteries give up to 31 hours of video on the inner display and 44 on the outer, with 50 percent charge in about 20 minutes. The FaceTime camera sits under the display at 1080p, and the page lists Touch ID rather than Face ID. Pre-orders open October 16, on sale October 23. No price on the product page.

*** Take
Seven years after the Galaxy Fold, Apple ships the version where the hinge is supposedly solved, and pays for it by moving biometrics backwards: an under-display camera means Touch ID instead of Face ID. That is the trade Apple normally refuses to make. The thread's real complaint is smaller and older: no SIM slot, which is still a problem in most of the world.

*** TLDR
An explainer on what the card networks actually are. Visa and Mastercard are not banks, issuers or processors: they route authorization messages, coordinate settlement between banks across borders, set the fee schedule that keeps everyone participating, and run the rulebook and dispute process. In the four-party model money flows cardholder to issuer to network to acquirer to merchant. On a $100 US purchase the merchant pays about 2.5 percent: the issuing bank keeps 2 percent as interchange, the processor keeps 0.35 percent, and Visa takes 0.15 percent as a network assessment. The EU caps interchange at 0.3 percent, which is why European rewards cards are thin. Visa held $11.2 billion in liquidity as of September 2024 against average daily settlement exposure of $84.3 billion, and charges $600 to review a dispute, $1,000 to appeal.

*** Take
The number that reframes the thread: Visa takes 15 cents of your $100, and your own bank takes $2. The card networks absorb the anger for a fee that mostly goes to the issuer, and rewards points are that interchange handed back to you, which is exactly why capping interchange at 0.3 percent deleted rewards cards in Europe. If you want lower fees, the thing to argue about is interchange, not the logo on the terminal.
