** Google no longer provides direct URLs in search results :google:search:privacy:scraping:

*** TLDR
Google Search now rewrites organic result links to google.com/goto?url=..., where the url parameter is a Google-specific encoding that cannot be decoded offline. The destination only appears in the Location header if you send a request back to Google for each result. Autom, a Google Search API vendor, says this has been consistent for logged-out and private sessions since late August 2026, and that it has updated its own pipeline to resolve the redirects. The obvious cost falls on scrapers, SEO tools and AI crawlers, who now need one extra request per link.

*** Take
Framing this as anti-scraping is convenient, because the same change also means Google sees a click on every result it shows you, including the ones you open in a new tab. The people writing the post sell a scraping API and have already worked around it in a day. The tracking survives.

*** TLDR
The argument is that pandas hits a wall somewhere between 10 and 100GB because it is eager, single-threaded and loads everything into memory, and that teams respond by jumping to Spark or Snowflake for data that still fits on one machine. The benchmark: a one billion row aggregation takes pandas 4m28s and 38.12GB of RAM, Polars 5.04s and 18.02GB, DuckDB 5.19s and 1.93GB. On a 3GB NYC taxi dataset DuckDB ran 93% faster using 67x less memory. The recommendation is Polars or DuckDB as the medium-data tier.

*** Take
The memory column is the real finding, not the wall-clock one. DuckDB doing the billion-row job in under 2GB is what kills the argument for spinning up a cluster. The title oversells it though: pandas is the interchange format half the ecosystem speaks, and nothing on that benchmark table replaces that.

*** TLDR
Operational notes from five years of running ClickHouse at Tinybird. Rough capacity math: a 32-core machine handles about 5GB/s of uncompressed data, so a 300TB table serving 1,000 QPS needs roughly 10 replicas. The most common failure is ingestion: too many small parts create a merge backlog, a heavy query eats the CPU, inserts pile up and the server OOMs. Zero-copy replication over S3 is described as buggy and capable of losing data. Format incompatibilities break upgrades two or three times a year. Other advice: keep a write-only replica, put ZooKeeper on separate hardware, hold sub-second query replicas under 40% load, default to ZSTD(1), and expect to read the source code. Above 20k rows/s with active schema changes, you need a full-time person.

*** Take
The honest line in the post is that a competent operator cuts hardware needs by three to four times. That is the actual pricing model for ClickHouse: cheap per byte, expensive per engineer, and the engineer is the part that is hard to hire.
